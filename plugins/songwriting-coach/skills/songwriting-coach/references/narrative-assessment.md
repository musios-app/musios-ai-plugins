# Narrative Assessment

*Does the song actually move*

Gathers every check concerned with story progression — whether the lyric goes somewhere across its sections, rather than restating one scene or feeling. Distinct from the Continuity Assessment (textual coherence: voice, tense, perspective) and from the Core Assessment (first-listen impressions).

- **Structural review** — does each section do distinct work, or repeat ground already covered? When a section is under-defined, meandering, or isn't earning its place — no clear rhythm, no hook, nothing memorable happening — the practical fix is usually to cut it rather than patch it line by line. Recommend cutting the section outright once that's the finding, rather than only flagging it as weak, unless the writer has a reason to keep it (a placeholder they intend to develop later, a deliberate quiet moment before a turn, etc.).
- **Verse-swap test** — could two same-role sections (e.g. verse 1 and verse 2) be swapped with nothing changing? If so, the second one isn't pulling its weight
- **Outro/resolution review** — does the ending feel earned relative to what the lyric set up?
- **Turn placement review** — is the emotional or narrative turn arriving at the right moment, or too early or too late?
- **Thematic through-line** — does the song's central idea hold across all sections, or does it drift?
- **Callback scan** — are recurring words, images, or phrases landing as intentional echoes or accidental repetition?
- **Narrative arc check** — does the lyric actually move (situation → change, beginning → middle → end), or is it static — one scene or feeling restated across sections with nothing progressing? Post-draft sibling to the pre-lyric Song idea check in Song Idea Assessment / Sharpening
- **List serves a bigger statement** — for a lyric built from a sequence of images or events (a "list song" or similar), does it cash out in an explicit or clearly implied point, or trail off as unexplained imagery?
- **Stakes check** — will a listener know why this song matters, and to whom? Assess whether the lyric makes legible what's at risk or what change is being sought, not just what happened. Sits here rather than in Core Assessment because "why it matters" is a question about whether meaning holds across the whole song — the same territory as Thematic through-line — not a first-listen gut reaction.

**Caveat affecting four tools above:** Narrative arc check, Verse-swap test, Outro/resolution review, and Turn placement review all presume the song is meant to progress — that stasis is a flaw, that verses should be non-interchangeable, that there's a setup/payoff or a turn to place. That presumption is often wrong (see Song mode taxonomy below): a mood piece, a thesis-and-variation song, or a pure list song can be static, repetitive-by-design, or turn-free on purpose, and that's not a defect. Until Song mode & direction (below) is promoted into the live Tool List, apply these four with judgment — ask whether progression was ever the goal before flagging its absence.

#### Song mode taxonomy (reference material — supports Song mode & direction below, not a tool itself)

The underlying test: **does time pass in the song, and do things change because of what happened earlier?** If yes, the song is narrative. If the song instead occupies one emotional or thematic space and explores it, it's non-narrative — whatever form that takes.

**Terms adjacent to "narrative":**
- Storytelling / narrative songwriting
- Story arc, plot
- Character study — built around a persona, not necessarily a plot
- Vignette — a small scene, not a full arc
- Concept song — organized around one idea/image rather than events

**Non-narrative structural forms:**
- **List song** — a catalog/inventory structure ("My Favorite Things," "We Didn't Start the Fire"); no plot, just accumulation
- **Mood piece / atmosphere song** — sustains a feeling or sonic space rather than telling a story (much ambient, shoegaze, some Sigur Rós)
- **Address/apostrophe song** — direct address to a person, god, or abstraction ("Dear ___," prayer-form songs); static situation, no plot movement
- **Argument/persuasion song** — makes a case or pleads a position (protest songs, many love songs — "why you should stay/leave/love me")
- **Snapshot/tableau song** — describes a single moment or image in detail, no before/after
- **Thesis-and-variation** — states an idea in the first verse, then explores it from different angles each verse without progressing it (common in folk and hymn forms)
- **Impressionistic/imagistic lyric** — juxtaposed images without causal or chronological connection (much Dylan, e.g. "Visions of Johanna"); meaning comes from resonance between images, not sequence
- **Refrain-driven/mantra song** — the hook is the content; verses are almost incidental scaffolding around a repeated phrase (much dance/pop, some gospel)
- **Emotional-state song** — describes/inhabits a feeling with no external events at all

**Genuinely non-narrative examples:** "Bohemian Rhapsody" (sectional, fragments of scenes rather than a story), "Where Is My Mind?" (imagistic, no plot), most house/EDM vocal hooks (pure refrain), Sigur Rós and much post-rock with vocals (mood over meaning), Cole Porter list songs, Gilbert & Sullivan patter songs.

#### Song mode & direction (draft — held from the live Tool List until finalised)

Not a check — there's no pass/fail verdict here. This is information-gathering about the writer's intent, run before the rest of the Narrative Assessment, so the four tools flagged above know whether their core question (does it progress) even applies to what the writer is trying to make. Ask the writer directly: **"What type of song do you have in mind?"** — and elicit, alongside the mode itself:

- **Genre(s)** — optional; don't press if the writer doesn't have one in mind
- **Audience response** — what response do you want from the audience? A list of target emotions (e.g. nostalgic, defiant, comforted, amused)
- **Music details** — key, tempo (e.g. BPM), rhythm/time signature (4/4, waltz, etc.) if known

**If ChatGPT has access to other songs by this writer** (in this project, or referenced elsewhere in the conversation), look at the genre(s) those songs sit in for a sense of the writer's typical pattern. Use this only to help with direction — e.g. surfacing what they tend to write as a starting point, or offering a contrast if they say they want to try something different — never as a critique, never as an expectation the new song should match their past pattern.

Has an echo at the pre-lyric stage too: a lighter version of this question could sit in Song Idea Assessment / Sharpening, asked before a draft exists — genre and target audience response established that early can shape Song Idea Development's generative tools directly (e.g. Prompt generator tailoring its prompts, Fresh angle finder aiming at a specific emotional response). This post-draft version then confirms the finished lyric actually executes the intended mode consistently, rather than drifting into accidental narrative shape when atmosphere was intended, or vice versa.

**Why this is background information, not just a Genre fit check input:** genre isn't only a content-comparison target — it changes what "correct" looks like for several other tools, because rhyme, rhythm, and register conventions vary sharply by genre (reggae's rhythmic patterns and loose rhyme tolerance are a different animal from jazz's, which differ again from pop or hymn form). When a genre is known, Beat review, Rhyme map, and Line-level stress test should calibrate their judgments against that genre's norms rather than applying one implicit standard across every lyric — see the cross-references added to those tools in the Sound and Rhythm Assessment (`references/sound-and-rhythm-assessment.md`). This is the main reason this whole cluster matters even while held from the live Tool List: the genre information it gathers is useful the moment a genre is known, independent of whether Genre fit check itself ever gets promoted.
