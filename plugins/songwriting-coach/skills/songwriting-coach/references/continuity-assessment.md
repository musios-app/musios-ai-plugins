# Continuity Assessment

*Keeping the whole song coherent*

- **Tense continuity** — does the song stay in one tense throughout, or does it shift (e.g. a past-tense verse moving to a present-tense chorus) as a deliberate choice rather than an unintentional slip? A tense shift can work well as a device — pulling a remembered moment into the present for the hook, say — but an accidental drift mid-verse reads as an error, not a device. Flag each shift and ask whether it was intended.
- **Pronoun/address continuity** — does the referent for "you," "she," "they," a name, etc. stay stable across the song, or does it drift so a listener loses track of who's being spoken to or about? Watch particularly for an ambiguous "you" that silently changes referent between sections (addressed to a lover in the verse, then to the listener in general in the chorus, without a signal that the address has shifted).
- **POV/person continuity** — does the song commit to one point of view — first-person confessional ("I"), second-person direct address ("you"), or third-person narrative ("she," "he," "they") — and hold it, or does it shift between them without a clear reason? A POV shift can be a legitimate structural device: a third-person verse that turns to direct second-person address in the chorus (the singer suddenly speaking to the subject of the song) is a common and effective move, and a first-person verse giving way to a spoken-to second person in a bridge can land as a deliberate turn. The test isn't "did it shift" but "does the shift do something" — does it mark a turn, a realization, a change in who's being addressed — versus reading as the writer losing track of who's telling the story. Distinct from Pronoun/address continuity: that one tracks whether a single pronoun keeps its referent; this one tracks whether the narrating position itself stays coherent.
- **Song fingerprint** — what the song keeps doing: recurring images, word choice and register, voice and stance, line and sentence shape, structural habits, emotional temperature, and any one odd move it repeats. Name three to five patterns, each seen at least three times or in two sections, with the sections and lines where it appears, what it does for the song, and how far it holds. Then note any section where a steady pattern drops out and ask whether that is the turn. Describe, don't rate: a pattern can be a strength or a habit worth a second look. With fewer than about eight lines or one section, say there isn't enough to see a pattern. Don't write lines or guess at the writer.
