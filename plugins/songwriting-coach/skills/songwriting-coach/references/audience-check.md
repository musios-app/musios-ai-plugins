# Audience check

*An attempt to read the lyric from the perspective of a person or group, through their eyes and ears. There is no guarantee the AI can project that perspective well.*

For a writer who wants to know how a lyric might land with someone unlike themselves: a different age, place, background or situation. ChatGPT reads or hears the lyric as that person, says what they would likely take from it, and then steps back out and tells the writer what that suggests. It is an attempt, and the AI's ability to take someone else's perspective is not guaranteed. The result is one rough, plausible reading, not a measurement and not a verdict. As a songwriter, you are encouraged to make reasonable requests that would not be offensive to listeners or other songwriters, and to interpret the AI's response with care.

This is different from **Listener lens** (Perspective Shifts Assessment), which asks how a generic first-time listener might receive a section. Here the writer chooses the listener, and the more specific the listener, the more useful the reading.

## How it runs

It is interactive, and it starts when the writer asks for it, in any of these ways:

- the name and a person: `audience check: a 30-something woman in the south-east US`
- the idea, in plain words: "through their eyes", "through their ears", "how would this sound to…", "what would a night-shift nurse make of this?"
- the name alone: "let's do an audience check", then answer "Whose perspective would you like to try?"

In replies, the check can be described as hearing the lyric *through their eyes / ears*.

1. **Ask whose perspective.** If the writer hasn't said who to read as, ask one question, in this form: "Whose perspective would you like to try? If you have an audience in mind, I'll start there." Then give this guidance, with the two examples in italics:

   > Adding specifics about the listener helps with the reading. Useful details are age range, where they live, what their life is like, and what music they listen to. Examples:
   > - *25-year-old club-goer in Berlin who likes house and disco*
   > - *45-year-old who loved 80s synth-pop and now mostly listens at home.*

   End the message with the note, as its last thing.

   **The note.** The first message of every use ends with this, in bold, word for word:

   > **NOTE: You are asked to use this feature with sensitivity to your audience, fellow songwriters, and the people behind this skill. I'll try to read your lyric from the perspective of the person or group you choose, but there are no guarantees how well I, as an AI, can project.**

   Show it every time the check starts, including each new listener in the same chat. It is not a one-off warning. If the writer named the listener up front, there is no question to ask: confirm the listener in one line, give the reading, and end that message with the note.

2. **Get a specific person.** A broad group is too wide to read through usefully. If the writer gives one ("a woman", "an older listener", "an American"), say so plainly and ask for more, giving an example of the level of detail that helps. For instance: "I'm less effective with a group as broad as that. Could you be more specific? For example, a US female college student." Useful details are age range, where they live, what their life is like, and what music they listen to.
   - If the answer is still broad, don't refuse. Offer two or three concrete people drawn from the request and the song, and let the writer pick or adjust.
   - If the writer has already said who the song is for, offer that listener first.

3. **Confirm the person in one line.** Restate who you are reading as, for example "30-something woman, south-eastern US", plus any assumption you're making that changes the reading. Then begin. Don't ask further questions unless the answer would change the reading.

4. **Read it as that person.** Cover the whole lyric or the section the writer chose; ask if it's unclear which. If no lyrics have been uploaded yet, follow the upload step first. Give:
   - **First impression**: what the song seems to be about and how it feels, in this listener's likely words.
   - **What lands**: lines, images or references that connect, and why.
   - **What puzzles or gets missed**: idioms, slang, cultural references or situations that may not translate.
   - **What could be misread**: lines that might mean something unintended from this position.
   - **What rings false**: anything this listener might feel a real person in the song wouldn't say or do.

5. **Step back out.** In a few lines, say what the reading suggests for the writer. Give questions or options, not rewrites; the writer decides whether any of it matters for their intended audience. Write no new lines unless the writer asks.

6. **Offer a next step.** Another listener, two listeners side by side for contrast, or the same listener once the writer has revised. Do one listener at a time.

## Keeping it honest

- **Open with the note, and label the result.** The bold note ends the first message, every time (step 1). The result is always a rough, plausible reading from a simulated listener, never a precise prediction. Never say "women think" or "older listeners feel". Real listeners vary, and the writer's own audience matters more than a simulation.
- **Read through the lyric, not a stereotype.** Base the reading on things the lyric actually prompts: references, idioms, situations, expectations, the sound of the language. Don't invent a personal history to fit the group, and don't assign taste or politics to a demographic.
- **Gender is the weakest lens.** Two people of the same gender can hear a lyric very differently. Offer it only when the writer asks, treat it like any other starting point, and ask for more detail alongside it.
- **Let the listener dislike it.** The point is an honest reading. Don't default to flattery or to finding the song relatable.
- **Sensitive positions.** A writer may want to hear a lyric from a position they haven't lived (a different culture, faith, disability, class or experience). Proceed with the same specificity and care, and say that this is an outsider's simulation. If the song depicts a community the writer doesn't belong to, suggest asking people from that community to read it. A simulation is no substitute.
- **Suggest real readers.** For any reading that matters, suggest the writer test the song on a real person who matches the listener they're writing for.
